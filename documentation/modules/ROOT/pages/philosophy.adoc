= Why Metarr?

NOTE: The short version lives in the README — this is the full story.

This page is my opportunity to explain my reasoning for why things are built the way they are in Metarr.

== Metadata is king

Metadata is usually the most valuable part of large systems. Our lives are increasingly defined by metadata in ways that might surprise you. 

Let's buy a toy on Huge-Online-Retailer.com. To you, it's a basic transaction.  Huge-Online-Retailer deposited a few dollars profit, you get your toy, you move on.  But, what if Huge-Online-Retailer doesn't just get the order, what if they also have your location when you placed the order.  That location metadata changes the transaction into something more interesting because it reveals you were standing in Brick-And-Mortar-Store at the time. Now Huge-Online-Retailer can gamble they have an exploitable advantage on that toy in that geographic area because one can reasonably assume Brick-And-Mortar-Store either costs more or doesn't have it. Huge-Online-Retailer.com automatically adjusts the real-time marketing on the toy, and over the next week, they end up selling a few more toys than they would otherwise because it turned out, Brick-And-Mortar-Store was out.  Now replicate this technique hundreds of thousands of times a day with millions of collection points and entire departments of some of the most skilled developers in the business dedicated to making it better, and it's easier to see how Huge-Online-Retailer knew you were pregnant before your mother did.

This is the bedrock of how all modern "rule your life" algorithms work — driven by multi-billion dollar metadata collection companies.

So, metadata is important and powerful, what does that have to do with a media collection? Simple: the data is the ones and zeros of the file itself; metadata is everything else. Even the file name is considered metadata because it tells you what's inside the file. Metadata includes all the information in the credits of the movie, cast, crew, production studios, then extends to poster images, background images, and more. All of this information is crucial to a media collection because this leads to Jellyfin knowing what poster to display in the UI.

But there are two problems. First, until now there was no way to pull it all together and manage it in a central location. Second, what you could do with it was limited by the creativity (and budget) of the developers.

== So what's different about Metarr?

Yes, I know — another *arr app claiming to be different. So what problem does Metarr actually solve? To explain, let's first describe a typical media server installation:

* A NAS sharing your files.
* Radarr pointing at that share looking for new movies.
* Jellyfin playing your movies.

Consider what's going on here from a metadata perspective.

* When you ask Radarr to download a movie, it calls TheMovieDB to get the metadata about that movie. This is how Radarr knows the cast, what the poster image is, and so on. When Radarr downloads the movie, it can optionally write that metadata to the directory — this is your NFO file and images. 
* Jellyfin has its own metadata management system that completely ignores Radarr. It can either manage the metadata in its own database or, optionally, write everything back to the media files directory.

So what is the system of record for the metadata? It's stored in Radarr, in Jellyfin, and on the shared drive — which copy is the truth? Metarr answers this question. It treats the files on your shared drive as the source of truth, manipulating those "records" (the files themselves) directly, then notifying other systems that something changed. This approach is eventually consistent.

All the downloaders in the *arr suite support writing metadata, but that capability is limited by the number of sources they use and the different formats involved. When you stack more systems on top of your media library — TV tuners, image systems, and the like — maintaining the metadata becomes nearly impossible. Applications like TinyMediaManager do an adequate job at the basics, but they have significant limitations.

== AI position

NOTE: Willie's personal opinion here.

I'm writing this out because of the major debate around AI coding agents and "vibe-coded" systems right now.  I need to clarify, right now I am talking about coding agents.  Creative Gen AI is a completely different conversation.

The argument usually boils down to: developers shouldn't or won't use AI because it can do horribly stupid things that cost time, money, or risk personal safety, and people simply wouldn't do stupid things like that. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AKN1Q5SjbeI["Well... yeah... okay.",window=_blank] Gen AI has been writing slop and bad code since around 2023. Developers have been writing slop and bad code since the 1960s. Flesh and blood developers are the ones that made https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_2000_problem[the Y2K Bug].  A flawed design pattern is integrated into every system imaginable by an entire generation of flesh and blood developers. Sure, AI can make mistakes and do unintended things.  Flesh-and-blood developers knowingly implemented a bug so stupid an entire civilization of people that didn't know the difference between COBOL and Portuguese, collectively went "How?!?!"  Crucially, AI can fix those mistakes faster than anything else in history, if guided properly.  So is your argument really about quality systems and safety, or more about the fact Claude just completed your entire sprint in 30 minutes and your manager knows it?

A developer programs computers. That's what they do.  The tools they use evolve and change over time.  And when the tools change, what programming is and what a developer does changes.

First, there was binary. Assembly was created to make writing binary in a human-readable language possible. C++ and COBOL-type languages were created because Assembly programs became incomprehensible quickly, and developers needed a language to abstract away assembly's complexity. Then the null-pointer, buffer-overrun, and stack overflow bug era began. It turned out that elite developers could manage application runtime well, but the average developer sucked because it was really hard and reference materials were sparse (out-of-date books with useless examples and magazines that ended with "to purchase a copy..."). On top of that, features took a lot of time to code because you had to do everything. Java arrived with its managed runtime, allowing developers to focus on functionality while the JVM handled the runtime. It turned out the JVM was much better at handling the runtime than the average C++ developer. Python is easier still because you don't have to worry about the runtime or care what types you are passing back and forth. Each major evolution in programming languages has moved developers one level further from binary, allowing them to focus more on functionality and less on implementation details, lowering the barrier to entry. Very few Python developers can open a hex editor and start coding in binary, and our modern world would be very different if they had to.

That's how to view Gen AI: it's an evolution of programming language that adds a new level of abstraction between the developer's intent and the ones and zeros. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper[Grace Hopper] designed COBOL's syntax and structure so business managers that still marveled over how quiet the latest typewriter was  could read code and verify their programmers were doing what they were told.  This will happen again. Something new will emerge that lets people program in structured natural language and run that through coding agents to generate functional apps. The language and stack used to implement it will become a configuration setting, receiving little attention.

Developers will remain developers. Their skills will evolve, and they will have tools to build systems the previous generation never imagined possible. You're reading its documentation right now. The code in this project represents years of handwritten work. Calling this project "vibe-coded" is inaccurate. Metarr's feature set was in development for years before the first directory was created, and the technology stack and architecture have been battle-tested in some of the most heavily used apps on the planet. I didn't build Metarr until recently because I know firsthand what it takes to build these types of systems. The Gen AI age has made it possible to create them without a team of dozens of developers. Focus on the functionality, not the plumbing. Sound familiar?

If you have issues with Gen AI software and choose not to use it or contribute to this project, I respect that completely. Gen AI is changing the world as I know it, and I'm uncertain how to think about it. But the simple truth is it's not going away, and the only alternative would be a venture capital-level investment of money and resources.
